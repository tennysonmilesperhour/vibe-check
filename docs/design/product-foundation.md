# Vibe Check: Free access to your own patterns

Updated September 8, 2026 from the user's feedback. The core free experience is implemented as of September 8, 2026. It takes precedence over earlier guidance that places cosmic profile setup or paid interpretation ahead of journaling and pattern access. The detailed direction below also includes future options. Current implementation includes check-ins and explicit drafts, private journaling and interaction labels, full history and filters, weekly/monthly reports, matched practices and feedback, report reflections, a private encrypted-export option, and session sign-out controls. Optional paid packaging, a separate PIN app lock, and AI integrations are not enabled.

## Intention

Help people see how their relationships, habits, and experiences accompany changes in how they feel over time, and return to what they recorded when their memory of a situation changes.

Use that history to recognize recurring stress patterns and recommend relevant practices that help the person move from what they experience as “not-self” toward “self.” In the free experience, the person defines this direction through their own values, needs, boundaries, and descriptions of feeling like themselves. The app helps them notice automatic responses and explore choices that feel more aligned.

A good day belongs in the record alongside the difficult days that preceded it. The app should make that whole history easy to see. People should be able to recognize recurring experiences, reflect on what they need, and make their own decisions with a clearer account of their life.

Access to that record, its charts, and useful weekly and monthly reviews is free for everyone. A person never needs to purchase a spiritual interpretation or connect an AI account to understand their own patterns.

## Daily reflection

Aim for a 30–60 second basic check-in. Ask “How did today feel?” using a consistent mood scale, then offer optional details: emotions, energy, sleep, activities or custom habits, people involved, and anything the user wants to remember. A short entry is complete. Longer writing is welcome.

Offer specific prompts when useful: “What happened?”, “How did it leave you feeling?”, and “What do you want to remember about this?” Let users record a meaningful interaction separately from their overall day. A pleasant afternoon should be able to coexist with a difficult interaction in the same day's record.

Optional relationship fields can capture whether an interaction felt supportive, strained, or unsafe, and whether a boundary was respected. These are the user's descriptions. Daily mood and interaction-specific feelings remain distinct in the data and charts.

For stress tracking, offer optional fields for perceived stress, bodily sensations, what happened just before, how the person responded, and what they needed or wanted to do. Ask “Did your response feel like you?” with room for uncertainty and the user's own words. Keep these optional so the basic check-in stays quick; never infer stress or alignment directly from mood.

Save drafts, support interrupted entries, and distinguish the time something happened from the time it was recorded. Let people correct entries and add earlier experiences. Missing days remain visibly unrecorded. Reminders are optional and have neutral notification text.

## The free orbit and pattern views

Keep the orbit as an inviting way to find people and recurring parts of life. Every visual element has an equivalent list item and opens a readable history. Names can be private labels; adding someone creates no invitation or notification to them.

Provide 7-day, 30-day, 90-day, yearly, all-time, and custom date ranges. Filters include a person, habit, emotion, and combinations where the data supports them. Long histories use pagination or aggregation while preserving access to every underlying entry.

Show variation and individual events alongside averages. Users can highlight entries they want to revisit. An event marked as unsafe stays visible in context even when the average mood is high. Display the number of recorded days, missing days, and the entries behind a comparison. An untagged day does not establish that the person or habit was absent.

Describe associations precisely: “On the days you recorded this habit…” or “In the entries you linked to this person…”. Distinguish daily mood from feelings about an interaction. Avoid presenting correlations as proof of cause, diagnosing a person from journal entries, or issuing a relationship compatibility verdict.

Illustrative example using invented interaction data:

> Today you described a good interaction. Of the 10 interactions you recorded with this person in the last 30 days, you marked seven as strained. You can read those entries together here. What stands out to you?

The user can follow the count to the ten entries and correct their labels. The app should support recognition without deciding what the user must do next.

## Recognizing stress patterns and returning to self

Follow the sequence the person recorded: **situation → bodily cues → response → need or value → chosen practice → later experience**. Missing parts stay unknown. Look across people, habits, settings, and times for repeated experiences, including exceptions and moments when the person responded differently.

Present a recurring pattern as a suggestion the user can confirm, rename, or dismiss. Show the dates, supporting entries, number of occurrences, and recording coverage. One event can prompt a reflection or a user-requested practice, but must not be described as a recurring pattern. Connect events across time only when the entries establish that sequence; timing alone does not prove a cause.

Use “not-self” for responses the person recognizes as moving away from their own needs or values. Use “self” for how they describe acting with greater choice, honesty, connection, or respect for their boundaries. Let them change these words or use plain-language alternatives. Do not assign a self/not-self identity from an algorithm, chakra, mood score, or personality system. An optional Human Design interpretation can add its explicitly labeled perspective without determining the baseline judgment.

Stress, anger, sadness, and disagreement can coexist with acting in alignment. A protective response in an unsafe situation must not count as personal failure. Track what the person says about agency and alignment separately from whether they felt calmer.

An illustrative report using invented entries:

> In three entries this week, you described feeling pressured to answer quickly, tension in your chest, and agreeing before checking what you wanted. You wrote that you want more time to decide. Does this connection fit your experience?

If the user agrees, recommend a brief pause and private rehearsal of a phrase such as “I'll get back to you,” using the practice rules below. A later reflection asks whether they noticed the cue, had room to choose, and felt their response respected what mattered to them. The user decides whether and where trying a different response is appropriate.

Weekly reports surface a small number of relevant stress patterns and a practice for the pattern the user wants to work with. Monthly reports revisit recurrence, exceptions, and the user's reported sense of choice and alignment, alongside practice responses. Show the whole history and gaps in feedback. Avoid a “percent healed,” “true self,” or authenticity score.

## Weekly and monthly reports

Reports are part of the free baseline and cover the complete selected period, including relevant journal writing. Show the dates and entry coverage at the top. Make reports available in the app when a period ends or on the next visit; access never depends on enabling notifications or email.

The proposed default week is Monday through Sunday in the user's local time zone, with a configurable week start. Monthly reports cover calendar months. Label an unfinished period as partial and distinguish it from a completed report.

| Report | What it contains | Optional reflection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | Mood variation; difficult and supportive moments; people and habits recorded; recurring stress patterns and other user tags or themes; representative passages linked to their entries; relevant somatic practices and responses to earlier suggestions. | “What repeated?”, “When did you feel like yourself?”, “What would you like to practice next week?” |
| Monthly | The month's timeline; comparisons with the previous month using their respective coverage; recurring stress patterns and exceptions; changes around people, habits, or a user-marked event; practice response history, reported alignment, and a suggested focus for the next month. | “What has changed?”, “Where did you have more choice?”, “What would you like to try or protect next month?” |

A theme must have identifiable source entries. Mark suggested themes as interpretations and let the user rename or dismiss them. Quote their words accurately. With sparse data, present the entries that exist and explain the coverage; do not invent a trend or block access to the report.

The baseline can use deterministic summaries, user tags, and selected journal passages. A paid model or external AI account must never be a prerequisite. When a service is unavailable, the factual report remains useful and accessible. Corrections and deletions must carry through to reports, charts, exports, and cached excerpts.

Weekly and monthly reflection is optional. Users do not have to complete another questionnaire to receive the report. Save their additional reflections with the period so they can revisit how their understanding changed.

## Somatic practice recommendations

Weekly and monthly reports include relevant practices for the user's intention of somatic reprogramming: noticing bodily responses and practicing different ways of responding over time. Public descriptions should use concrete language such as “body-based practice.” Avoid promises to cure trauma, reset the nervous system, or permanently rewire a response.

The weekly report offers one or two manageable options matched to a recorded stress pattern and the response the person wants to practice. Match the situation, bodily cues, user-defined intention, available context, and past practice preferences. Explain the connection so the user can assess it. The monthly report revisits those experiences and invites the user to continue, adapt, or choose another practice. With too little context, offer a clearly labeled general option or let the user select a focus; do not invent a personal pattern.

Each recommendation includes:

- **Why it appears:** a brief observation linked to the relevant entries, or the user's chosen intention. The user can correct or dismiss the connection.
- **What to try:** a named practice, its purpose, a short duration such as one to three minutes, and complete instructions. Offer seated, eyes-open, or sensory alternatives where relevant.
- **When it might fit:** a context the user can choose and a flexible suggestion for repetition. Every practice can be skipped or stopped.
- **How it felt:** an optional before-and-after reflection, including “more settled,” “about the same,” or “more unsettled,” plus space for the user's own words. Separately ask whether they felt more able to choose a response that fit their needs or values; a calmer feeling alone does not establish that.

Proposed examples illustrate matching and copy; they are not validated personalized treatments:

| Recorded experience | Possible practice invitation |
| --- | --- |
| “I felt on edge after these conversations.” | Brief orientation: when the user chooses a suitable moment, notice a few neutral objects or sounds nearby. Offer a sensory alternative and keep attention outside the body if preferred. |
| “My shoulders felt tense after work.” | A short, comfortable movement break with a seated option and no requirement to stretch into discomfort. |
| “I kept agreeing before I knew what I wanted.” | Pause in a comfortable position and privately rehearse “I need time to think.” The invitation does not require confronting anyone. |

Save the practice, date, source report, and any user response in the free history. Later reports can say, for example, “You tried this four times and reported feeling more settled twice.” Distinguish attempts without feedback from reported outcomes. Do not infer effectiveness from completion counts or claim a practice caused a mood change. Let users hide a practice, record an unwelcome response, and prevent repeat suggestions. Corrections and deletions carry through to these summaries too.

Use a reviewed practice library with sources and clear suitability limits; matching can work without paid AI. The WHO includes grounding among its everyday stress-management skills. The VA notes that relaxation can sometimes increase distress, which supports offering alternatives and an easy way to stop. These sources do not validate Vibe Check's proposed matching system or establish a “reprogramming” outcome. See [WHO: Doing What Matters in Times of Stress](https://www.who.int/thailand/activities/doing-what-matters-in-times-of-stress) and [VA: Coping with Traumatic Stress Reactions](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/gethelp/coping_stress_reactions.asp).

Recommendations must not infer a trauma diagnosis or nervous-system state from mood scores, relationship tags, or chakras. Avoid unsupervised trauma exposure, forced breathwork, or instructions to push through distress. When an entry describes feeling unsafe, preserve access to the history and user-controlled support options; a calming practice must never imply that the situation is safe or that the user should learn to tolerate mistreatment.

### Actions for what someone is feeling now

Provide a free “Help me with what I'm feeling” entry point from Today, check-ins, and reports. A person can choose confusion, fight or flight, anger, shutdown, emotional numbness, procrastination, or “mixed / unsure,” and describe another experience in their own words. These are user-selected descriptions, not diagnoses or automatic nervous-system classifications. Confusion here means feeling mentally tangled or overwhelmed; numbness means feeling emotionally disconnected.

Offer one short action at a time, with a visible duration, an alternative, and a stop option. Start with the person's current selection; use their confirmed patterns and practice feedback to refine suggestions. Access requires neither a completed journal entry nor enough history for a report. Keep the plants' introduction brief and narration optional so someone can reach an action with little reading.

The following are proposed starting options for the reviewed practice library. Their purpose is to help someone find more clarity, connection, or choice; no action guarantees a state will disappear.

| What the person selects | First action to offer | Possible next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confusion / overwhelm | Take a brief pause and notice one neutral detail nearby. Write one sentence about what is happening and one about what remains unclear. | Choose one small question or next step. Offer permission to postpone a nonurgent decision. |
| Fight or flight / feeling on edge | With eyes open if comfortable, notice a few neutral objects or sounds and the support of the chair or ground. Offer comfortable, unforced breathing as an optional alternative; no breath holds or forced deep breaths. | Ask what would offer more space or support now. Do not announce that the person is safe based on completing a practice. |
| Anger | Pause before sending a message or responding. If appropriate and safe, make some space from the interaction and try a short walk or comfortable seated movement. | Privately name what felt wrong and what boundary or response the person wants. Offer a draft they can keep private; never prompt an immediate confrontation. |
| Shutdown / feeling unable to act | Begin with one small, comfortable movement, such as moving the hands or adjusting position, and notice one thing in the room. | Choose one manageable care action or seek support from someone the person trusts. Make rest an available choice. |
| Emotional numbness / disconnection | Try a familiar, neutral sensory experience, such as noticing the texture of a cloth or listening to a familiar sound. Ask what, if anything, they notice without requiring an emotional response. | Offer a gentle activity or a user-chosen connection with someone trusted. Avoid intense sensation or instructions to force a feeling. |
| Procrastination / difficulty starting | Ask what feels in the way: an unclear task, tiredness, worry, distraction, or something else. Define the smallest visible first action, such as opening a document and writing its title. | Offer an optional five-minute start, then reassess. Depending on the person's answer, suggest clarification, rest, or practical help. Do not assume every delay comes from stress. |

The anger options draw on the NHS's suggestions to allow time before reacting and use movement or support. Breaking tasks into smaller steps and trying a brief work period come from CCI's practical procrastination guidance. These sources inform the individual options, not a claim that Vibe Check's state matching has been validated. See [NHS: Anger](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/anger/) and [CCI: Practical Strategies to Stop Procrastination](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/-/media/CCI/Mental-Health-Professionals/Procrastination/Procrastination---Information-Sheets/Procrastination-Information-Sheet---04---Practical-Strategies-to-Stop-Procrastinating.pdf). Grounding options use the WHO and VA references above.

After an action, ask “What changed, if anything?” Offer relevant responses such as clearer, more connected, more able to begin, more settled, unchanged, or more uncomfortable, with free text and skip. Save feedback only when the person chooses to record it. Weekly and monthly reports can revisit the state, context, action, and response, including occasions when it did not help. A user can dismiss an action or choose another without escalating its intensity. The movement toward self remains defined by their own needs and choices.

## The plants' voice

The [plant voice](plant-voice.md) and [Nature Sanctuary](nature-sanctuary.md) remain the character and setting of the app. The plants speak together as one guide. They first help the user record, notice, remember, and reflect, and introduce other systems when the user chooses them.

The plants' observations stay close to the person's words and the visible data. They can ask about a boundary or invite a pause. They must not reframe mistreatment as a spiritual lesson the user needs to endure, dismiss a harmful event as incompatibility, or use a positive reading to contradict the user's account.

The free experience should feel complete and cared for. A user who never opens Cosmos still receives the app's central benefit. Optional plant companions add a distinct reflective perspective when someone wants to go further.

## Privacy for relationship journaling

Private by default means entries remain within the account, sharing is an explicit user action, names can be nicknames, and notifications reveal neither names nor journal content. Spoken plant narration starts only when requested. Provide a free optional app lock, clear session controls, and a straightforward explanation of storage, syncing, and export behavior.

Before sharing, let the user select dates and entries, remove names, and preview the complete report. Make it clear whether the result is a downloaded file or an externally shared resource. Do not automatically email reports, contact anyone in the orbit, or send entries to an AI provider.

These requirements are informed by NNEDV's guidance on app privacy and device access. An app lock cannot make a monitored device safe; describe that limitation accurately in contextual help. Offer discreet access to support resources, with the user controlling whether to open them. See [Safety First for App Developers](https://www.techsafety.org/blog/2017/8/30/so-you-wanna-build-an-app-safety-first) and [Choosing and Using Apps](https://www.techsafety.org/choosingapps).

## Optional AI interoperability

The user's interest in connecting a personal AI is exploratory. Vibe Check should remain provider-neutral and useful without ChatGPT or any other assistant. The welcome experience should explain Vibe Check's own value.

Begin with a portable export that a user can choose to use elsewhere. Investigate direct integrations only when the relevant provider supports them. Before an external transfer, show the provider, selected information, and any cost or retention implications. Let the user exclude names and sensitive entries and revoke an ongoing connection. No automatic journal syncing or assumed account compatibility.

## Implementation priorities and acceptance

The inspected checkout already has check-ins, people, charts, and a computed weekly overview. Its Patterns page requests 120 recent entries and offers 30/60/90-day ranges. The weekly component emphasizes average mood and the brightest day. These behaviors need expansion to meet this direction; the document does not claim those changes are shipped.

1. Make the free journal, people/habit history, and long-range charts complete. Preserve both difficult and positive entries and distinguish missing data.
2. Build weekly and monthly reports from the full period, including journal content, source links, an archive, stress pattern recognition, practices matched to those patterns, and user-reported response and alignment history. Make the same practice library accessible immediately for user-selected states. Ensure both paths work without paid AI.
3. Complete privacy controls and previewable, selective exports alongside the core experience.
4. Apply the plants' voice to the entire free journey, then add optional paid system depth and consider AI interoperability.

Accept the baseline when a free user can record an experience, revisit it months later from a person or habit chart, understand what a report is based on, and export the record. A sequence of difficult entries followed by one good day must retain the full sequence in charts and reports. Cancelling a paid upgrade must preserve those capabilities.

For practices, a free user must be able to understand why a suggestion appeared, read all instructions, skip or try it, record their response, and revisit that response in later reports. A hidden or distressing practice must not keep returning as a recommendation.

For immediate help, a new user with no journal history must be able to select each of the six starting states, open an appropriate action, choose an alternative or stop, and optionally record what changed. A mixed or uncertain state must also have a usable path. The app must not label every state as fight or flight or offer the same breathing exercise for every selection.

For stress patterns, the user must be able to trace a suggested pattern to its entries, correct or dismiss it, define what a more aligned response means to them, and see later experiences without the app assigning an identity or treating lower stress as proof of progress toward self.
